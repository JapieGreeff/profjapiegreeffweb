SA Game Jam 2017: A Collection of Moments

A solo game jam entry exploring life, choices, and the collection of random things we accomplish.

The Concept

For the 2017 SA Game Jam, I decided to go it alone—not entirely by choice, but I was happy to lock myself in a room for a weekend of coding. The designation was a bit tricky; at the time, I was officially a student but leading a team of researchers at the Serious Games Institute at NWU. The theme led me to a concept where your whole life is really just building up to a collection of random things you accomplish, and then you die. Not to be depressing, but that's the loop!

Gameplay Mechanics

You start off in your house and immediately begin to age. Depending on where you choose to go—home, work, the gym, the club, or the classroom—you lose stats on one of your gauges: Social Life, Money, Career, Education/Knowledge, or Health. At each location, interactions drive some gauges up and others down. For example, working hard at your keyboard boosts your Money gauge but drains your Health and Social Life.

Balancing Act

As you age, your health bar depletes faster and faster. The game features three different end conditions: Death by Illness (Health drops to zero), Suicide due to Depression (Social drops to zero), or Suicide by Bankruptcy (Finance drops to zero). It's a sombre commentary on how difficult it is to balance all aspects of life, let alone excel in all of them.

The End Game

In the end, your tombstone shows the collection of experiences and traits you managed to gain over your lifetime, summed up as a list. I was particularly proud of the state machine implementation for managing these events. I attempted to have the events stated in the past tense on the tombstone by doing code replacement, though language proved trickier than code!

Reflections

This was one of my best jams ever. Despite a rocky start, I was very happy with the end results. I felt like the "King of State Machines" by the end! The community feedback was invaluable—players noted the poignancy of the concept, comparing it to experimental narrative games. Some found the UI confusing initially, confusing the location labels with the stats they affected, which was a great lesson in UI/UX design for me.

I eventually uploaded a final build that fixed some of the initial confusion. It was a great learning experience; I realized I'm definitely a programmer at heart, not visual designer!
